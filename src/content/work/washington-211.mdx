<p class="label">TL;DR</p>

When someone needs housing immediately, a search that returns the wrong results isn't a small problem. We tested users with traumatic brain injuries (TBI) and discovered that they couldn't find help that fit them. Our heuristic evaluation showed that the same issues affect everyone.

I worked across usability testing and synthesizing, then led the design and prototyping: mapping how Washington 211's search fails users and designing solutions that address the highest-priority breakdowns.

<p class="label">Background</p>

## People couldn't find information, so they kept calling

Washington 211 is a nonprofit that points people across the state to essential services. Many callers were asking about information already on its website, overwhelming the call centers. Because its users have such different needs, we partnered with the Department of Social & Health Services to design for many of them at once.

<video src="/work/washington-211/background.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline aria-label="The Washington 211 website"></video>

<p class="label">Our initial assumption</p>

## We believed people with traumatic brain injuries (TBI) were giving up because results didn’t fit them

People with TBI kept landing on services they couldn’t use, so we set out to understand what kept them from reaching support.

<div class="callout">
  <p class="label">Our north star</p>
  <p>How do we make results fit what people search for, and pages easier to follow, so people with TBI can reach the support they’re eligible for?</p>
</div>

<p class="label">Research</p>

## Watching people search for help

We watched 6 people with TBI work through 4 tasks on Washington 211, recruited from 41 screener respondents, guided by DSHS’s research on TBI, and paired with a heuristic evaluation of the site.

<p class="label">Process</p>

## The problem was never just about TBI

Our heuristic evaluation found 21 design and interaction requirements rated major or critical, with accessibility the most severe, so every decision below was made for all of Washington 211’s users.

<div class="process">
<div class="process-counter" aria-hidden="true">01</div>
<div class="process-steps">
<section id="process-step-1">

### Widening from TBI to everyone

What we thought was a TBI problem was everyone’s problem. The only thing our problem statement needed to change was who it described.

<div class="callout">
  <p class="label">Our actual problem statement</p>
  <p>Users with traumatic brain injuries struggle to identify eligible services when presented with ambiguous or non-applicable options. This exposure to irrelevant options causes frustration and forces users to abandon their search before securing valid support.</p>
</div>

</section>
<section id="process-step-2">

### Mapping why search fails

Before designing any screens, I mapped how a search moves through Washington 211 today, from landing on the site to reaching a result. Two breakdowns stood out: people have to go through three steps and a jump to a separate site before they can type anything, and exact keyword matching means anyone who doesn’t use 211’s own labels gets nothing useful back.

<section id="process-step-3">

### Deciding what to fix first

Given we had 21 recommendations and limited resources, we sorted each one by impact and effort, so Washington 211 could start with the fixes that help most for the least work.

<p class="label">Reflections</p>

<div class="split">
  <h3>The best pattern isn’t<br />always the right one</h3>
  <p>We flagged the “safe exit” button as a bug because it sent people to Microsoft Edge. Washington 211 explained it exists so people in abusive situations can leave the site quickly. A pattern that looks wrong in a heuristic evaluation can be protecting someone, and context from stakeholders changed what we recommended.</p>
  <h3>Pivot, pivot, pivot</h3>
  <p>We started this project knowing that we had to make additional adjustments to make our usability tests as minimally cognitive taxing as possible. We had pilot tests with DSHS and did extensive research on the matter. It was still not enough. We had to remain flexible among technical difficulties, surprising findings, and a high drop-out rate.</p>
</div>
